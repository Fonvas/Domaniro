How to Spot Low-Quality Link Prospects: 7 Red Flags Before You Pitch

A rejected pitch costs you a few minutes. A link from the wrong site can cost you more. Here are the warning signs worth checking before you reach out.

When you're building a list of sites to pitch for guest posts, resource links, or partnerships, the goal isn't the longest list, it's the right one. Sites built mainly to sell or swap links rarely pass real value on, and being associated with them is a risk you don't need. These seven red flags will help you filter them out early.

1. A score that doesn't match the site's apparent size

Start with a quick authority check. A very low score on a site that claims to be an established publication is worth a closer look. The reverse deserves scrutiny too: a surprisingly high score on a site with little content or traffic-worthy pages can mean its links are not what they seem. A mismatch between the numbers and what you see on the page is the first signal to dig deeper.

2. Very few referring domains for the score

As covered in our guide to referring domains, a large pile of links from a handful of sources is a weaker, more suspicious profile than the same number spread across many unique sites. If the referring-domain count looks small next to the score, be cautious.

3. Content on every topic at once

A site that publishes about travel, crypto, dentistry, and home loans with equal enthusiasm usually isn't serving a real audience. Genuine sites tend to have a recognizable focus. A scattered topic mix is a common trait of sites that exist to host paid placements.

4. "Write for us" pages that mention payment

Guest contributions are normal. But a page that openly advertises pricing for posts, packages for "sponsored" placement with followed links, or guaranteed authority metrics is a sign that links are the product. Editorial sites decide on quality, not on a price list.

5. Thin, repetitive, or obviously generated articles

Open a few recent posts. If they're short, say little, repeat the same phrases, or read as if rewritten from other sources, visitors won't value them and neither will search engines. Pay attention too to how many outbound links each post carries and where they point.

6. Little sign of a real audience or author

Look for dated posts, named authors with some history, a working contact page, and signs that people actually engage, such as comments, shares, or mentions elsewhere. A site with none of this may exist only for its links.

7. A page full of outbound links to unrelated sites

If the page you'd be placed on links out heavily to unrelated sites, your link will sit in a crowd that signals nothing. A handful of relevant, editorially placed links is a very different environment from a long list of commercial anchors.

A fast triage workflow

You don't need a long audit for every prospect. First, scan the score badges on a search results page and set aside the clearly weak ones. Second, check referring domains for the ones that remain. Third, spend two minutes reading the site itself against the red flags above. Anything that passes all three is worth pitching.
