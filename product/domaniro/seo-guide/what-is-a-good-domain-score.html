What Is a Good Domain Score? How to Benchmark Against Competitors

"Is a 4 good? Is a 7 good enough?" The honest answer is: it depends on who you're competing with. Here's how to turn a raw score into a useful benchmark.

One of the most common questions after checking a domain's authority score is the simplest one: is that number good? Unfortunately, no score from any provider comes with a universal pass mark. A 4 might be excellent for a local plumber and underwhelming for a national software company. The number only becomes meaningful once you have something to compare it to.

Why there's no universal "good" score

Authority-style scores are built from how many sites link to a domain and how established those sites are. Because different industries have very different link ecosystems, the typical score in one niche can look nothing like the typical score in another. A niche dominated by large media publishers and well-funded brands pushes the baseline up. A niche made up mostly of small local businesses keeps it low — and a modest score can still be a strong one there.

This is also why chasing a specific number is a trap. A score is a fast estimate of relative weight, not a target to hit, and it isn't a direct ranking factor on its own.

The benchmarking method: compare against the page-one sites

The most reliable way to judge a score is to look at the domains that already rank for the searches you care about. A simple process:

  1. Pick one or two target searches. Choose the queries you'd actually like to rank or earn links for.
  2. Note the Domain Score of each organic result on page one. Skip ads and obvious giants (large marketplaces, encyclopedias, major publishers) that rank for almost everything.
  3. Find the range. What's the lowest score on page one? What's the typical mid-range? That spread is your realistic benchmark.
  4. Place your own domain, or your prospect, on that range. Below the range means a gap to close. Inside it means you're competitive. Above it means authority probably isn't what's holding you back.

Look at all three numbers, not only the score

A score on its own can hide a lot. When you benchmark, glance at the global rank and the referring domain count too. Two sites with similar scores can have very different referring-domain counts, and that difference often tells you which one has a broader, more durable set of links behind it.

Don't ignore the outliers

If a low-score site is ranking on page one, that's useful information, not noise. It usually means the page is a particularly good match for the search, the competition for that query is thin, or the site has strengths a link-based score can't see. Those are often the most realistic opportunities for a newer or smaller site.

A simple rule of thumb

Instead of asking "is my score good," ask "is my score in the range of the sites I'm trying to compete with?" If yes, keep going. If you're well below, focus on earning links from a wider set of unique, relevant sites, since referring domains tend to move a score more than piling up links from the same few sources.
